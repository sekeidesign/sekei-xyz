The standard process for managing RAIDs is old school and manual, <Highlight>often taking place in spreadsheets or SharePoint lists</Highlight>. This turns already overwhelmed PMs into bottlenecks, since the entire team has to wait for them to review and update the log to get latest picture of how their project is doing.

To help empower our customers to manage their RAIDs more effectively, I kicked things off on the project by doing a deep dive into existing RAID logs that our customers were maintaining, usually in spreadsheets. 
I then did a deeper dive into RAID management best practices, researching documentation and how existing industry standard tools like SAP handle RAID management. Here's what I learned.

## Anatomy of a RAID log

<Highlight>A RAID log is an interconnected collection of Risks, Action Items, Issues, and Decisions.</Highlight>
It documents the status of a project as it evolves, identifying threats to
the critical path and ensuring appropriate ownership, mitigation, and traceability.

In digital transformation programs, where projects span three to five
years, cross project dependencies, large teams, and even larger budgets
make a RAID log critical to success and extremely difficult to manage.

<RaidTypes />

As these projects evolve and grow, Risks begin to crop up. During their lifecycle, the PM's primary goal is to monitor them, and mitigate them as they rise in likelihood and impact.

If the team is unable to adequately address a risk as it intensifies, it will eventually become an Issue. This means the Risk has been realized and is actively driving the project off the critical path.

By modelling this lifecycle and the associated relationships, we were armed with a framework to not only build a RAID log, but to also build a set of natural human-in-the-loop workflows including:
* Status evolution based on relationships
* Risk promotion to Issue
* Automated follow-ups and escalation

## Extraction and human-in-the-loop

RAIDs crop up in meetings, chat messages, emails, reports, and any other
surface where people interact. They get discussed and tackled in an even
broader set of tools, and their evolution is long and rarely clear-cut.
This complexity is <Highlight>an opportunity for Tato to put its deep project
knowledge, integrations, and agents to work</Highlight> to extract, monitor, and
document RAID items across the lifecycle of a multi-year project.

<ExtractionFlow />

Meetings are the primary source of RAID items, but the items themselves
are usually managed in Jira, Smartsheet, or plain old spreadsheets. Our
first job was consolidating those sources into a normalized log that could
act as a single source of truth. Tato updates the log after any activity,
and pulls from external tools periodically or on command through MCPs and
bespoke integrations.

The RAID updates on the activity summary page started as a simple section
showing the under-the-hood thinking of our agent. <Highlight>Exposing that machinery
almost always overloads the user</Highlight>, and despite our best
efforts, LLMs produce volume, which leads to an overwhelming number of
items.

Hiding the machinery was the first improvement, and it cut a lot of noise
on its own, since a single meeting could produce upwards of 60 snippets for only a handful of RAID items. 
From there I reduced the amount of text and added icons and
avatars, which improved legibility and hierarchy and made the whole list
easier to parse.

<NeedsReview />

I also exposed two primary actions on each item, so tracking or dismissing
is always within reach and the log stays tidy. These actions will also have downstream implications on the status of the item.

<RiskTriage />

Dismissing an item tucks it away, letting the system know this AI extraction was not useful.
Tracking an item marks it as exceptionally relevant, which tells our agent to pay special attention to it moving forward. I used a subtle interaction to make this key action feel impactful and energetic.

<TrackPress />


This first iteration landed well, with early feedback showing a perception
that Tato was smarter and more nuanced. We've found <Highlight>our users' perception of quality to be just as important as the quantitative improvements when it comes to interacting with an LLM.</Highlight>

I then reworked the entire
summary page into a two column layout, giving RAID
management its own first class surface. The user keeps meeting
context in view while digging into items for proper grooming.

## An auditable paper trail

A well managed RAID log is also an accurate ledger of a project's
evolution, and of what the team did to mitigate critical risks. Our
history view was hard to reach, sometimes hidden behind multiple
clicks, and it focused on diffs between versions. Diffs matter, but they
fail to paint the most important picture: what caused a change, and when.

Clarity matters even more when working alongside an agent. The history
documents which changes the agent made and the reasoning behind them, so
the human can understand them and correct them when necessary.

<HistoryDrawer />

I tried many iterations for showing an item's history and ultimately kept
the drawer we already had. The drawer offers the most real estate for
understanding a risk and its history while letting the user stay in their
current workflow, moving easily between every item in a list. Making it
more visual and tightening the hierarchy also gave space back and
increased legibility.

## The ultimate control centre

The last piece of this iteration was an improved control centre. Much of
the work happens in the context of an activity summary, and the components
and flows are largely shared, but we still wanted a delightful surface for
managing the full log.

<RaidTable />

I started with basic UX improvements: tightening space for
more breathing room, grouping related controls, and making the table
contents scrollable rather than the page, so the header, footer, and every available
action stay visible. I also ensured the table had robust and intuitive keyboard navigation, including the skip to main content link, row navigation with arrow keys, proper focus management, and more.

Finally, I built a collection of indicators to show impact, likelihood, and status. All animated with state machines to support smooth transitions between any number of states.

Although our user base is fairly small, especially when it comes to PMs who actually manage RAID, we've seen strong signals of adoption and value, including a 5x increase in items logged via chat in the 4 weeks since launch. <Highlight> Chat is one of the main ways users can generate new RAIDs and import existing items via MCP or document uploads. </Highlight>

We've also received positive qualitative feedback from early users, including:

<Quote cite="PM on an $8M ERP implementation">
	"The new RAID, I love it. Once I saw I could trust it, I got
	rid of my SharePoint lists and centralised everything there."
</Quote>

This RAID log is one small step toward a future where humans and AI
collaborate to ensure successful business transformations. The next step is to build upon our learnings and double down on clever automations that help large teams close the loop on their RAIDs.
