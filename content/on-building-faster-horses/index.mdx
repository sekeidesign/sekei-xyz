If you’ve ever worked on a team building a product, you’ve likely heard a quote along the lines of

<Quote>
“If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.”
</Quote>

It’s misattributed to Henry Ford, who not only didn’t say this, but didn’t even invent the car. His greatest differentiator was the moving production line, which allowed him to produce cars cheaper and faster. It’s more than likely that many of his customers would have asked for cheaper, more readily available cars had they been asked.

Regardless of the dubious provenance of this quote, there is an important yet deeply nuanced truth written between the lines.

I want to address this nugget of truth, while addressing the dangerous traps that surround it. Traps that, in my experience, guide product leaders down a path of self-righteousness, and a path that veers further from product-market fit, not closer to it.

In the age of cheap, fast software production, it is more important than ever to dismiss the quote’s falsehoods, and to internalize its truths.

## Your customers are not product experts

The core truth that many correctly identify within this statement is that customers tend not to be experts at building products.

Whether you’re building a car, a coffee machine, or AI software for digital transformation programs, your ICP is in most cases exceptional at their job, and terrible at yours.

It is your privilege and duty to protect them from their own misguided requests; to refrain from injecting their horse with steroids or strapping jet engines to its hooves in an attempt to make it faster.

## You are not your customer

While the truth above is undeniable, the conclusion that many defend using this quote is categorically wrong.

It goes something like “Customers don’t know what they want, therefore we cannot look for direction in their feedback, and we must minimize acting on their requests.”

Once again, there is truth hidden deep within that conclusion. A locksmith who gets to work by horse, when asked what they want, will jump to solutions and tell you that what they desperately need is a faster horse.

If you, as a product builder, then go on to devote the next year engineering the most aerodynamic saddle, or the most energizing brew of coca leaves for horses, you will have inched towards solving the users problem. You will have also developed a creative solution to a poorly formed problem space; a shallow understanding of the user’s latent demand.

## The nuanced middle ground

While a customer may not be able to accurately express their deeper need, we’ve concluded that it is not their job to be a well-articulated product builder. It is not their job to dig deep into their psyche and understand what the true underlying problem they’re trying to solve is.

They’re just trying to get to their destination faster.

When a customer asks for a faster horse, we must listen attentively.

We must ask them where they go with their horse. How long it takes them to get there. If they get dirty from the horse galloping in the mud and whether that bothers them. Whether they’ve explored other alternatives like moving closer to their destination. Whether they always travel alone on horseback or if they take passengers and cargo with them on the horse.

We must understand their life deeply, so that we may understand their problem in context, and so that we may be able to develop new and creative solutions to existing and well-defined problems.

If your instinct is to say “my users don’t know what they want” and conclude that you can safely ignore their feedback and build what you think is best then congratulations: you’ve just bought yourself several months of wasted time, resources, and morale.
